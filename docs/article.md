# The price of overnight money

> standfirst: How the Fed sets America's most important interest rate, and why its next move rarely surprises anyone

On September 16th 2026 the Federal Reserve raised interest rates by a quarter of a percentage point, to 3.75-4.00%. It was the Fed's first rise since 2023 and the first under Kevin Warsh, its chairman since May. Markets had expected it for weeks. The rate it moved is set in an odd corner of finance.

Every working day America's Federal Home Loan Banks find cash sitting idle at the Fed. The eleven banks, cooperatives that fund mortgage lenders, keep accounts at the Fed but, unlike ordinary banks, are barred by law from earning interest on them. So they lend the money out overnight, without collateral, to whoever pays most. The borrowers are mostly the American branches of foreign banks. They park the cash straight back at the Fed, which does pay them interest, and keep the difference, a few hundredths of a percentage point.

The price the two sides agree on is the federal funds rate.

## What it is

The fed-funds rate is what banks pay to borrow reserves, the money they keep in their accounts at the central bank, for one night with nothing pledged against the loan. Most other prices in American finance are set by reference to it.

Two numbers share the name. The Federal Open Market Committee, the Fed's rate-setting body, announces a target range. The market then settles somewhere inside it, and that is the effective federal funds rate, or EFFR. Since March 2016 the New York Fed has calculated it as the median of the trades banks report each day, weighted by volume.

The market is small and odd. The home-loan banks supply more than 90% of the lending. Foreign branches do most of the borrowing because, unlike American banks, most pay no deposit-insurance fees. Daily volume, about $110bn in 2023, is a trifle beside the Treasury market. Two narrow groups of lenders and borrowers, neither doing anything a textbook would call banking, set a rate that prices credit around the world.

## Who sets it

Twelve people vote. Seven are governors of the Federal Reserve Board, nominated by the president and confirmed by the Senate. The president of the New York Fed holds a permanent eighth seat and serves as the committee's vice-chair. The other four seats rotate each year among the remaining eleven regional Fed presidents.

The committee meets eight times a year, about six weeks apart. Each meeting ends with a statement at 2pm and a short set of instructions to the New York Fed's trading desk, known simply as the Desk, on how to make the new rate stick. Four times a year members also publish forecasts, including, since 2012, the "dot plot": one unnamed dot per member marking where each thinks the rate should be in coming years. The committee calls the dots forecasts; markets often treat them as promises.

Since January 2019 the chairman has held a press conference after every meeting.

## How it works

The Fed does not, strictly, set the fed-funds rate. It sets a price that it does not charge, in a market where it does not trade. And the way it does so has changed completely since 2008.

::: figure corridor_vs_floor
title: Two ways to set a price
subtitle: Schematic: interest rate against the quantity of reserves, before and after October 2008
source: Source: Federal Reserve
:::

**Before 2008, reserves were scarce.** Money left at the Fed earned nothing, so banks held as little as they could: enough to settle payments and meet their legal requirements, and no more. Small changes in the supply of reserves therefore moved the price a lot. Each morning the Desk lent or borrowed against Treasury bonds for short periods, in deals called repos, adjusting the stock of reserves until the rate hit a single target. A ceiling sat above: the discount rate, which the Fed charges banks that borrow from it directly.

> **Repo.** A short loan secured by securities, usually Treasuries. The borrower sells the securities and agrees to buy them back, often the next day, at a slightly higher price. The difference is the interest. When the Fed does a repo it lends cash, adding reserves.

**Since 2008, reserves have been ample.** Congress let the Fed pay interest on reserves from October 1st 2008, and the bond-buying that followed filled banks' accounts. Reserves once counted in tens of billions of dollars now run to trillions, so adding or draining a few billion no longer moves the price. Instead the Fed steers the market with rates that it simply announces and that anyone eligible can take.

::: figure administered_rates
title: Inside the band
subtitle: United States, daily policy rates, %; the discount rate marks the ceiling
source: Sources: Federal Reserve; FRED
:::

The first is interest on reserve balances (IORB), known before July 2021 as interest on excess reserves (IOER). No bank will lend for less than it can earn, without risk, by leaving the money at the Fed, so this rate sets a floor under what banks charge.

The floor leaks, because the biggest lenders in the market are not banks. The home-loan banks and money-market funds hold cash but cannot earn interest on reserves, and a lender who cannot reach the floor will lend below it. So in September 2013 the Fed started letting these firms deposit cash with it overnight, through its overnight reverse-repo facility (ON RRP), at a lower rate it also sets. That rate is the true floor for the lenders, which is why the effective rate usually sits *below* the rate the Fed pays banks, not above it.

> **Reverse repo.** The same trade the other way round. The Fed borrows cash overnight and hands over Treasuries as collateral, draining reserves. The lender gets its cash back the next morning, plus interest.

Two more facilities cap the range from above. The standing repo facility, created in 2021, lends cash against Treasuries at a fixed rate to any eligible firm. The discount window charges the same, but banks shun it: borrowing there suggests a bank had nowhere else to go. The committee formally adopted this system in January 2019.

On September 21st 2026 the target range stood at 3.75-4.00%. The Fed paid banks 3.90% on reserves and non-banks 3.75% on reverse repos. The standing repo facility and the discount window both charged 4.00%. The effective rate was 3.88%.

The system can still break. On September 17th 2019 corporate tax payments and a big settlement of Treasury sales drained cash from banks on the same morning. Repo rates briefly touched 10%, and the fed-funds rate followed them out of its range. The Desk had to lend $75bn a day at first, more later, to pull it back.

## How it spreads

One night's borrowing changes nobody's investment plans. The overnight rate matters because longer rates depend on where investors expect it to go. A ten-year Treasury yield is roughly the average short rate investors expect over the next decade, plus a premium for tying money up that long.

The nearest rates move almost in step. Treasury bills, commercial paper and floating-rate business loans track the fed-funds rate closely. Banks set their prime rate at the top of the target range plus three percentage points, 7.00% today, and price credit cards and home-equity loans off it. Mortgages are the exception. The 30-year mortgage rate follows the ten-year Treasury yield, not the overnight rate, so mortgages can get cheaper even while the Fed raises rates.

Further out, the rate moves bond yields, share prices and the dollar, and through them spending, then hiring and, last, prices. It reaches them slowly. Milton Friedman called the delays "long and variable", and the description still holds. Common estimates put the biggest effect on output and inflation 12 to 18 months after a change. A committee voting today is acting on an economy it will not see for a year or more.

## How it got here

America built its central bank late and reluctantly. The panic of 1907 left it the only big financial power without one, and Congress passed the Federal Reserve Act in 1913. During and after the second world war it held down Treasury yields at the government's request, until the Treasury-Fed Accord of 1951 gave it the independence it has today.

Independence alone did not bring low inflation. Inflation rose from about 1% in 1964 to more than 12% in 1974, while Arthur Burns, chairman from 1970, kept rates low under pressure from Richard Nixon. In October 1979, at a Saturday-evening meeting, Paul Volcker, the Fed's chairman, changed tack. Rather than steer the fed-funds rate, the Fed would control the quantity of reserves and let the rate go wherever that took it. It went to about 20%. Unemployment hit 10.8%, but inflation, which peaked at nearly 15% in early 1980, was below 5% by late 1982. The chart below shows the pattern since 1960: the Fed raises rates when inflation runs hot and cuts when unemployment jumps.

The change that matters most for anyone studying the data came later and quietly. Before February 1994 the Fed announced nothing. Traders inferred its decisions from the Desk's trading, and often guessed wrong. Many changes came between scheduled meetings. In February 1994, under Alan Greenspan, the committee announced a change for the first time. Since 2000 it has issued a statement after every meeting. The record of announced decisions is therefore just over 30 years old.

::: figure target_rate_history

That record spans the calm of the "Great Moderation"; the cut to zero on December 16th 2008 and the years of bond-buying that followed; the first rise in nearly a decade on December 16th 2015; the return to zero on the evening of Sunday March 15th 2020; and then 5.25 percentage points of rises between March 2022 and July 2023, the fastest since Volcker. Under Ben Bernanke the Fed adopted a formal 2% inflation target in 2012. In August 2025 the Fed dropped the framework it had adopted in 2020, under which it promised to make up for past misses on inflation and to respond only to shortfalls in employment. September's rise, with inflation still above target, was Mr Warsh's first move.

## The predictable Fed

The Fed has become easy to forecast, and it has done so on purpose. Before each meeting it tells markets, in speeches, minutes and statements, what it is likely to do. We built two statistical models of its decisions to see what they respond to and how well they can be predicted. Once you know what the Fed did last, monthly economic data add little or nothing to a forecast. Market prices on the eve of a meeting forecast best of all, but mostly because they echo the Fed's own signals.

### Keeping score

Because most decisions are holds, a model that always says "hold" is usually right, so accuracy tells you little. We use a probability score instead. Each model puts a probability on each of the five kinds of decision; the score measures how far those bets land from what happened, and punishes bets far from the truth more than near misses. Like golf, lower is better. We report it mainly as the percentage improvement over a baseline that simply forecasts the share of each decision among all earlier meetings.

We test the models forward in time, as a forecaster would have faced them: each of 171 meetings from December 2005 to September 2026 is predicted from earlier meetings alone. Holds make up 73% of these test meetings, more than across the whole record, because the window includes the years when rates sat at zero and could not be cut, about two-fifths of the meetings. Scoring only the meetings away from zero leaves the ranking of the models unchanged. The economic inputs are revised figures lagged a month, not the figures the committee saw at the time, which if anything flatters the rules and the economic data. The Methods section below gives the details.

### What the Fed responds to

The first model is the standard one. The Fed picks a target for its rate from inflation and the jobs market, then moves only part of the way towards it each quarter. In five of the six versions we fit, the model finds a clear response to the jobs market: for each point that unemployment rises above its natural rate, the Fed eventually cuts by about 1.7 points. Its response to inflation is less clear. Every estimate has the right sign, but none can rule out a Fed that merely keeps pace with inflation. Because the rate moves so slowly, the model struggles to tell the Fed's response to inflation from its habit of staying put.

### What the Fed will do next

The second model predicts which of the five kinds of decision the committee will take at each meeting. We feed it four kinds of input: the Fed's recent history (its last decision, the time since the rate last moved and whether the meeting was unscheduled); 11 monthly series on inflation, jobs, output and financial stress; the gaps between the actual rate and five policy rules the Fed publishes; and the yields on three- and six-month Treasury bills the day before each meeting.

The best-known of those rules, John Taylor's of 1993, sets the rate from inflation and the gap between output and its potential. It tracks the actual rate in calm times and parts company in crises and at the zero bound.

**History does most of the work.** With its three history inputs alone, the model scores 38% better than the baseline.

**Economic data add little once you know the history.** Added to it, they cut the model's improvement over the baseline from 38% to 14%. Part of that damage comes from asking a few hundred meetings to pin down 11 extra inputs. Restraining the model so it cannot chase noise shrinks the damage, but still leaves it at 28%, worse than history alone. A leaner version, adding only core inflation and payrolls, reaches 33%: still no gain. Alone, economic data score 29% worse than the baseline. The Fed's rules do little better: added to history, they cut the improvement to 31%, a gap small enough to be noise on 171 meetings.

**Market prices do best.** The two bill yields alone score 56% better than the baseline; with history, 60%. But the yields are taken on the eve of each meeting, after the Fed has signalled what it plans to do. They mostly echo the Fed's own words.

Fed-funds futures, which pay out on the average overnight rate in a given month, show this most starkly. Taken minutes before each announcement, their prices miss one decision in 113 between 2010 and 2023: on March 3rd 2020, at an unscheduled meeting, they priced a quarter-point cut and the Fed cut by half a point. On those meetings futures score 96% better than the baseline, against 62% for bill yields. That measures how well the Fed communicates, not how well an outsider can forecast it weeks ahead.

### The cut nobody sees

**The history model never correctly calls a 25bp cut: it scores nought out of eleven.**

The eleven small cuts in the test window came in three groups. The three in 2007-08 came as a recession began; only the last fell inside it. The three in 2019 were insurance, made with the jobs market still strong. The five in 2024-25 were billed as recalibration, trimming rates as inflation cooled. Compared with big cuts, small ones come as payrolls grow by 86,000 a month rather than shrink by 81,000, with the VIX, a gauge of expected stockmarket turbulence, at 21 rather than 28. Just 5% come at unscheduled meetings, against 37% of big cuts, and 19% during recessions (as later dated), against 42%.

**Yet the two kinds of cut still overlap.** Assigning each cut to whichever group's average it sits nearer, leaving it out of that average, sorts 62.5% of them correctly, against 52.5% by guessing the more common kind.

::: figure cut25_misses
title: Small surprises
subtitle: Fed 25bp rate cuts, by the model's predicted decision, Dec 2005-Sep 2026
source: Sources: Federal Reserve; FRED; our analysis

Of the eleven misses, the model calls eight holds and three big cuts, and it never gives a small cut more than a 29% chance. It ranks small cuts above chance but never on top. On the day, such meetings look like the holds either side of them. We tuned nothing to these eleven cases.

Markets see a little more. The bill-yield models each call two of the eleven, and never give a small cut more than a 46% chance. Both call the three 2019 cuts holds, with 81-91% confidence. Futures, whose data cover only those three of the eleven, call all three correctly. The published economic data do not flag a small cut in advance; the Fed's own signals in the days before do.

### What this does and does not show

It does not show that the Fed ignores the economy. Both models are simple statistical fits, and the Fed's recent history absorbs everything persistent, including the slow-moving economic conditions that took the rate to where it already is. A committee that responded quickly and fully to the economy could produce a rate series much like this one. Nor does it show that monthly data are useless to traders: market prices already reflect them, along with everything else traders know.

It does show that the Fed's next move is rarely a surprise, because the Fed makes sure of it. Its recent behaviour tells you nearly everything monthly economic data can. Market prices tell you more, chiefly because they carry the Fed's own signals.

## Methods

### Scores

Accuracy aside, we compute five scores:

- **Weighted kappa** measures agreement with the actual decisions beyond what chance would give, and punishes a miss more the further it lands from the truth.
- **The ranked probability score**, the probability score in the body, rewards a model for putting its probability on the right outcome and on outcomes near it. Lower is better.
- **The area under the ROC (receiver operating characteristic) curve** asks, for each kind of decision, how often the model ranks a meeting that took it above one that did not. Always saying hold scores 0.500.
- **The area under the precision-recall (PR) curve** asks the same question but punishes false alarms on rare decisions harder. Always saying hold scores the share of each decision, 0.200 on average.
- **F1** balances how many of each decision the model catches against how many of its calls are right.

The last three are computed for each of the five kinds of decision against the rest and then averaged, so a 25bp cut counts as much as a hold.

Every input is lagged a month: consumer prices for one month appear only in the middle of the next. The inputs are revised data, not the real-time data Athanasios Orphanides (2001) showed fit rules much worse; the lag does not reproduce real-time data, and the revised figures, if anything, flatter the rules and the economic data. The economic series come from FRED (Federal Reserve Economic Data), run by the St Louis Fed.

### Model one: specification

The Fed picks a target for its rate based on inflation and the state of the jobs market, then moves only part of the way towards it at each step:

$$
i_t = \rho\, i_{t-1} + (1-\rho)\left[r^* + \pi_t + a\,(\pi_t - \pi^*) + b\, g_t\right] + \varepsilon_t
$$

Here $i_t$ is the policy rate, $\pi_t$ core inflation, $\pi^* = 2\%$ the Fed's target, $r^*$ the neutral real rate and $g_t$ the jobs-market gap: the natural rate of unemployment minus the actual rate, positive when the jobs market is tight. $\rho$ measures inertia. $a$ is the response to inflation beyond one-for-one, so the rate rises by $1 + a$ points in the long run for each point of inflation, and $b$ is the response to the jobs market. Subtracting $r^* + \pi^*$ from both sides gives the form we estimate by least squares:

$$
i_t - r^* - \pi^* = c_1\,(i_{t-1} - r^* - \pi^*) + c_2\,(\pi_t - \pi^*) + c_3\, g_t + \varepsilon_t
$$

so that

$$
\rho = c_1, \qquad a = \frac{c_2}{1-\rho} - 1, \qquad b = \frac{c_3}{1-\rho}.
$$

We estimate it on quarterly data from 1983, with standard errors robust to the correlation between neighbouring quarters.

| | Estimate | Standard error |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\rho$, inertia | 0.934 | 0.023 |
| $a$, inflation response | 1.04 | 0.78 |
| $b$, jobs-market response | 1.69 | 0.58 |

$n = 175$, $R^2 = 0.972$.

Inertia and the response to the jobs market are clear. The response to inflation has the right sign but lies just 1.3 standard errors from zero. The model recovers $a$ and $b$ by dividing by $1-\rho$, and as $\rho$ nears one the divisor shrinks and takes the precision with it. The coefficients estimated before that division are all tight: $c_1 = 0.934$ (0.023), $c_2 = 0.135$ (0.056) and $c_3 = 0.112$ (0.030).

The specification chart shows six versions: three starting dates, each fitted on monthly data (grey) and quarterly data (blue). Every inflation bar crosses zero, and the bars widen as the sample shortens. All six jobs-market estimates lie between 1.4 and 1.9, and all but one bar clear zero.

Standard errors in brackets.

The monthly estimate from 1994 implies that, in the long run, the Fed raises rates by $1 + a \approx 5$ points for every point of inflation. That is division by almost zero, not a finding. Monthly data make the problem worse: the Fed meets eight times a year and usually holds, so most months show no change and $\rho$ is pushed towards one. We report quarterly estimates, as John Taylor, Richard Clarida, Jordi Galí, Mark Gertler and Glenn Rudebusch did.

### Model one: rolling windows

The rolling estimates use ten years of monthly data. Across windows ending before 1983, the median $a$ is −0.61. For windows ending between 1983 and 1999 the median is +0.78. This reproduces the result of Clarida, Galí and Gertler on our data: the Fed did not lean against inflation before Volcker, and did afterwards. After 2000 median $\rho$ climbs from 0.93 to 0.98, and the division by $1-\rho$ makes the estimates swing wildly, so the chart omits those windows.

### Model one: chairmen and a random walk

Estimates for individual chairmen fail. For every chairman since Greenspan, $\rho$ comes out at or above one and the division blows up: Jerome Powell's $a$ is −20.2, with a standard error of 66. Only Greenspan's 221 months yield usable figures: $\rho = 0.949$, $a = 0.75$ and $b = 2.38$. The code declines to report the others.

Against a random walk, the model ties. Forecasting one month ahead, its root-mean-square error is 0.548 against 0.539 for simply assuming no change, a ratio of 1.015. It wins only after 2000, with a ratio of 0.982. That is almost built in: a rate that does not move in most months is nearly a random walk over a month.

### Model two: inputs

Model two is an ordered logit, a regression suited to ranked outcomes, refitted before every test meeting. Shuffling the data for cross-validation, a common shortcut, would leak the future into the past. The baseline is the share of each kind of decision among the meetings before each one.

*History.* The Fed moves in small steps and seldom reverses soon after a move. Michael Dueker (1999) and Liang Hu and Peter Phillips (2004) modelled changes in the target as ordered choices that depend on the previous change. James Hamilton and Òscar Jordà (2002) added the time since the rate last moved. Our history inputs follow them.

*Rules and the economy.* Taylor (1993) showed that a simple rule tracked the Fed's decisions from 1987 to 1992. Since 2017 the Fed has published the prescriptions of several rules in its twice-yearly Monetary Policy Report. Our rule gaps are the distance between the actual rate and five of them: Taylor (1993), balanced approach, shortfalls, inertial and first difference.

*Market rates.* Hamilton and Jordà found that the spread of the six-month Treasury-bill rate over the target helped predict changes. Joachim Grammig and Kerstin Kehrle (2008) built on their model. Our market inputs are the three- and six-month bill rates on the last trading day before each meeting, less the previous target.

*Futures.* Kenneth Kuttner (2001) showed how to read the expected change at a single meeting from fed-funds futures. Refet Gürkaynak, Brian Sack and Eric Swanson (2007) found that futures beat other market measures at forecasting the rate over the next few months. CME Group's FedWatch tool turns futures prices into a probability for each outcome by splitting the expected change between the two nearest quarter-point steps; we do the same. Our prices come from Michael Bauer and Eric Swanson (2023), via the San Francisco Fed, and are taken minutes before each announcement. They run from January 2010 to December 2023.

### Caveats

- The neutral real rate, $r^*$, is fixed at 2%. A time-varying estimate from Thomas Laubach and John Williams, later with Kathryn Holston, is available in the code but untested in these models.
- Records of dissenting votes start in March 2002. Earlier dissents sit only in the minutes, so dissent is not an input.
- The market and futures inputs use prices taken after the Fed's pre-meeting signals: the last trading day before each meeting for bills, minutes before the announcement for futures.

> **IOER** interest on excess reserves: the name of IORB before July 2021.  
> **IORB** interest on reserve balances: the rate the Fed pays banks on money held at the Fed.  
> **NBER** National Bureau of Economic Research: dates America's recessions.  
> **ON RRP** overnight reverse-repo facility: where money funds and other firms lend cash to the Fed overnight, against Treasuries.  
> **PR** precision-recall.  
> **ROC** receiver operating characteristic.  
> **VIX** a gauge of expected stockmarket turbulence, derived from options prices.
