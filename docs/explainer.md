# The price of overnight money

Every working day the Federal Home Loan Banks — eleven cooperatives that fund America's mortgage lenders — find cash sitting idle in their accounts at the Federal Reserve. They hold those accounts but, unlike banks, may not earn interest on them. So they lend the money out overnight, unsecured, for whatever the market will pay, because any positive rate beats nothing. On the other side stand the American branches of foreign banks. They borrow the cash, park it straight back at the Fed, and keep the difference.

The price those two groups agree on is the federal funds rate.

## 1. What it is

The fed-funds rate is the cost of borrowing reserve balances — the money banks keep in their accounts at the central bank — for one night, with no collateral pledged against the loan. It is the price of overnight money in America, and almost every other price in finance is built on top of it.

Two numbers go by the name. The Federal Open Market Committee, the Fed's rate-setting body, announces a target range. The market then clears somewhere inside that range, and where it clears is the effective federal funds rate, or EFFR. Since March 1st 2016 the Federal Reserve Bank of New York has calculated the EFFR as the volume-weighted median of the trades that banks report each day on a return called form FR 2420. Before that it used the volume-weighted mean of trades arranged by brokers, a narrower and noisier sample.

The market itself is small and odd. The home-loan banks supply more than 90 per cent of daily lending volume. Trading ran at roughly $150bn-175bn a night before 2008, fell to $60bn-80bn through the 2010s and recovered to about $110bn in 2023 — a rounding error next to the Treasury market. An arbitrage between two narrow groups of institutions — neither of them doing anything a textbook would recognise as banking — sets a rate that prices credit across the world.

## 2. Who sets it

Twelve people vote. Seven are governors of the Federal Reserve Board, nominated by the president and confirmed by the Senate. The eighth is the president of the New York Fed, who holds a permanent seat and serves as the committee's vice-chair. The remaining four seats rotate on a fixed one-year cycle among the other eleven regional Fed presidents. All eleven attend every meeting and argue their case; only four of them cast a vote in any given year.

The committee meets eight times a year, roughly six weeks apart. Each meeting yields a statement at 2pm and an Implementation Note, a short set of instructions telling the New York Fed's trading desk — everyone calls it the Desk — what to do to make the announced rate real. Four times a year the Fed also publishes a Summary of Economic Projections, which since January 2012 has included the dot plot: one dot per participant, marking where each thinks the rate ought to sit at the end of each of the next few years. The dots carry no names, and they are projections rather than promises, a distinction the committee repeats often and markets ignore regularly.

The chairman began holding press conferences in March 2011, at first only four times a year. Since January 2019 one follows every meeting.

## 3. The plumbing

Most explainers say the Fed sets the fed-funds rate. It does not. The Fed sets a price it does not itself charge, in a market it does not itself trade in, and the way it pulls that off has changed completely since 2008.

![Two ways to set a price](../figures/production/corridor_vs_floor.png)

**Before 2008, reserves were scarce.** Money left at the Fed earned nothing, so banks held as little of it as they could get away with — enough to settle payments and meet their requirements, and not a dollar more. Demand for reserves was therefore steep: shave the supply a little and the overnight rate jumped, add a little and it fell. The Desk exploited that steepness. Every morning it bought or sold securities, mostly through repurchase agreements — repos, which are short-term loans made against Treasury collateral — to nudge the quantity of reserves until the rate landed on a single point target. A ceiling sat above: the discount rate, charged to banks that borrow from the Fed directly, which from January 2003 was fixed a percentage point above the target.

**Since 2008, reserves have been ample.** Congress let the Fed pay interest on reserves from October 1st 2008, and the bond-buying programmes that followed flooded the banking system with them. Where reserves were once counted in tens of billions of dollars, they are now counted in trillions. That moved the market onto the flat part of the demand curve, where adding or draining reserves does nothing to the price. Open-market operations lost their grip. Control passed to a set of rates the Fed simply announces, and which anyone eligible can take.

![Inside the band](../figures/production/administered_rates.png)

Interest on reserve balances, IORB, is the first of them. No bank will lend at less than it can earn risk-free by leaving the money at the Fed, so IORB puts a floor under what banks charge.

The floor leaks, though, because the biggest lenders in the market are not banks. The home-loan banks and the money-market funds hold cash but cannot earn IORB on it, and a lender who cannot reach the floor will go under it. The Fed's answer is the overnight reverse repurchase facility — the ON RRP, relaunched in September 2013 — which lets those non-banks park cash at the Fed overnight at a rate the Fed sets. That is the reservation rate for the people who actually do the lending, and it sits below IORB. Which is why the effective rate prints *below* the rate the Fed pays banks, rather than above it. Accounts of the system that describe IORB as the floor for the market, rather than for banks alone, get this backwards.

Above the range sit two more facilities. The standing repo facility, opened in August 2021, lends cash against Treasuries to anyone eligible at a fixed rate, capping how far the market can rise. The discount window sits above that and is blunted by stigma: a bank that uses it fears being read as a bank that had nowhere else to go. The committee adopted this framework formally on January 30th 2019, and has said it intends to keep it.

As of September 21st 2026 the configuration reads: target range 3.75-4.00 per cent, IORB 3.90, the reverse-repo rate 3.75, the standing repo facility 4.00, the discount rate 4.00, and the effective rate printing at 3.88.

The floor does not hold itself. On September 17th 2019 a corporate tax date and a large Treasury settlement drained cash from the system on the same morning. Repo rates spiked more than 300 basis points — hundredths of a percentage point — above the top of the target range, touching 10 per cent at one point during the day, and the fed-funds rate followed them out of the range. The Desk had to inject as much as $100bn a day to bring it back. Plumbing is not a figure of speech here.

## 4. How it travels

An overnight rate should not matter much. One night's borrowing changes nobody's investment plans. It matters because every longer rate is priced off expectations of it: a ten-year yield is roughly the average short rate investors expect over the next ten years, plus a term premium — the extra return they demand for tying money up. The Fed moves the first term directly and hopes the second behaves.

The nearest instruments move almost in lockstep. Treasury bills, commercial paper and floating-rate business loans track the fed-funds rate closely. The prime rate is mechanical: banks set it at the top of the target range plus three percentage points, so 7.00 per cent today, and credit-card rates and home-equity lines are quoted as a spread over it. Mortgages are the famous exception. The 30-year mortgage rate follows the ten-year Treasury yield, not the overnight rate, which is why mortgages can get cheaper while the Fed is raising rates — a fact that surprises homebuyers every cycle.

Past the rates themselves, the effects widen and blur. Investors rebalancing portfolios push the change into corporate bond yields and equity valuations. Higher returns on dollar assets pull the exchange rate up. Through all of these channels the rate reaches spending, hiring and, last of all, prices.

It reaches them slowly. Milton Friedman's phrase for the delay, "long and variable lags", is still the honest description. Working estimates put the peak effect on output and inflation at 12 to 18 months. A meta-analysis of the empirical literature finds an average of 29 months to the peak effect on prices, and economists at the Fed have defended figures anywhere from 9 months to 36. A committee voting this afternoon is acting on an economy it will not see for a year or more, and will not be able to grade its own decision until long after the political argument about it has finished.

## 5. How it got here

America built its central bank late and reluctantly. The Panic of 1907 left it the only major financial power without one, and the Federal Reserve Act followed in 1913. For its first four decades the Fed was not fully its own master: during and after the second world war it held Treasury bond yields down at the Treasury's request. The Treasury-Fed Accord of 1951 ended that arrangement and is the birth date of operational independence.

Independence did not, on its own, deliver low inflation. Inflation climbed from about 1 per cent in 1964 to over 12 per cent in 1974 and roughly 15 per cent by early 1980. In October 1979, at a Saturday-night meeting, Paul Volcker changed the target: instead of steering the fed-funds rate, the Fed would control the quantity of non-borrowed reserves and let the rate go wherever that took it. It went to about 20 per cent. The prime rate hit 21.5 per cent, unemployment reached 10.8 per cent, and inflation was under 5 per cent by late 1982.

![Thirty years of decisions](../figures/production/target_rate_history.png)

The change that matters most for anyone studying the data came later and made no noise at all. Before February 1994 the Fed announced nothing. Traders worked out what had been decided by watching what the Desk did the next morning, and often got it wrong. Of 55 target changes between 1987 and 1994, only seven happened at a scheduled meeting. Then in February 1994 the committee announced a rate change for the first time; by 1997 it put an actual number in its directive; from 2000 it has issued a statement after every meeting. A continuous record of announced decisions is therefore almost exactly thirty years old, which is where the chart above begins and why.

What that record holds: the calm of the Great Moderation; the collapse to zero on December 16th 2008 and the years of bond-buying that followed; the first increase in nearly a decade on December 16th 2015; the return to zero on Sunday evening, March 15th 2020; then 525 basis points of increases between March 2022 and July 2023, the fastest run since Volcker. In August 2025 the Fed abandoned the flexible average-inflation-targeting framework it had adopted in 2020, dropping both the promise to make up for past misses and the pledge to respond only to shortfalls in employment. Kevin Warsh became chairman in May 2026. On September 16th 2026, with inflation still above target, the committee raised rates a quarter-point to 3.75-4.00 per cent — its first increase since 2023.

Thirty years, eight meetings a year, twelve votes each time. The rest of this project asks what the pattern of those decisions looks like when you lay them end to end.
